**What is happening?**  
In Jean-Paul Rodrigue's model of an asset bubble there are four phases. Firstly, the stealth phase where smart money enters with high returns and little public attention. Then as institutional money flows in, media coverage begins and the awareness phase starts. In the case of AI, this is when hedge funds like the highly leveraged Situational Awareness fund made its 439% return. (Forbes, 2026a) Then in the mania phase, institutional investors begin to sell and retail volume surges. These retail investors pile in based on price momentum and FOMO rather than fundamental analysis of what they are putting their money into. Finally, the market crashes due to a catalyst triggering a selloff.

The stage we are in right now in the semiconductor trade is mania. With that comes the inflow of retail investing and the outflow of institutional investments. In July, we saw the outflow of Situational Awareness, a fund which suffered a sharp July selloff, faced margin calls and sold a large portion of its public equity portfolio to Citadel. (Forbes, 2026a) On the other hand, retail investors put $12B into US Semiconductor ETFs in June 2026, ten times as much as June 2025. (Bloomberg Finance L.P, 2026) With the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index falling by 25% since then, the question is whether this is just a part of the mania or an omen for the crash.

**What are the risks of a market concentrated by retail investing?**
In the last few weeks, we can see the risks of a market concentrated by retail investing with the KOSPI, the benchmark stock market index of South Korea. Following an earnings miss, SK Hynix, which accounts for around a quarter of the index, lost over half of its value in roughly one month since its late June peak. (BBC, 2026) On 23 June 2026, the KOSPI plunged 10% in a single session with Samsung falling by over 12%. (Forbes, 2026b) William Pesek commented on the volatility of the KOSPI describing it as trading more like a meme stock or a 1970s economic upstart rather than a world-class stock exchange. (Forbes, 2026c) The main reason for this volatility is the concentration of retail investing. Retail investors account for a large proportion of the market which brings the intrinsic risk of fickleness. Their share of daily trading volume fell from a peak of 37.3% to 31.2%, reflecting this fickleness. (Seoul Economic Daily, 2026) In South Korea, the situation is made worse by a large proportion of investors leveraging their investments - meaning when their investments fall, they feel the loss 2x or 3x more than other investors. This makes them more likely to exit at the first sign of a correction.

**Could we be wrong?**
Retail investing now accounts for ~20% of US stock market volume and NYU Stern research states that the median retail investor spends just six minutes researching a stock before trading. (Forbes, 2025) However, this idea that retail investors are impulsive may be outdated.

Bill Capuzzi for the Forbes Financial Council argues that compared to five years ago, retail investing is less emotional and less driven by social media. (Forbes, 2026d) This shift reflects the emergence of zero-commission trading which allows continuous instead of episodic participation. Theoretically, this should make investors more structured and data-driven in their stock picking. However, does this not just result in the same problem? With the rise of AI in stock picking and shared public information, investors' behaviour becomes more similar. (Forbes, 2026d)
This means more concentrated volatility, not less.
