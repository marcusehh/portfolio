**What is GPU-backed lending?**  
It is a new form of asset-backed lending where the collateral isn't a building, a fleet, or a company's cash flow, it's a stack of Nvidia chips. A data-centre operator wants to buy thousands of GPUs to rent out to AI labs; a lender advances the cash and takes a security interest in the hardware itself, sometimes through a special-purpose vehicle that holds legal title to the chips and leases them onward. It sounds niche. It isn't. Over $11bn has already been lent to "neoclouds" pledging Nvidia chips as collateral, and CoreWeave, the company that pioneered the model, saw its GPU-backed debt grow from $2.3bn in 2023 to $7.5bn by 2024\. (Introl, 2026\)

**Why are companies turning to it?**  
Because the capital required to build AI infrastructure has outgrown what conventional lending was built for. Morgan Stanley estimates a $1.5tn financing gap in data-centre buildout through 2029, and Clifford Chance puts total data-centre capex at roughly $6.7tn by 2030\. (Clifford Chance, 2026\) Banks have been reluctant to fund this directly. A GPU is a fast-depreciating asset, losing an estimated 30–40% of its value in year one as newer chips arrive, which makes it a poor match for the multi-decade loan structures banks are used to writing against buildings or infrastructure. (Introl, 2026\) Specialist lenders and off-balance-sheet vehicles have stepped into that gap.

**Who's actually doing it?**  
Nvidia itself, increasingly. In June 2026, an Australian operator called SharonAI signed a deal to scale up to 40,000 of Nvidia's newest chips over six years, backed by a "take-or-pay" commitment from Nvidia guaranteeing minimum revenue on the hardware. (SemiAnalysis, 2026\) That is the chip supplier underwriting demand for its own product. Analysts have started calling the underlying formula the "AI Project Trinity": a large GPU cluster, a creditworthy customer on a long-term contract, and a lender willing to structure debt against the chips. (EnkiAI, 2026\)

**Is this new?**  
Not entirely. Manufacturers financing purchases of their own products is a century-old practice known as captive or vendor finance. GM built GMAC to sell cars; John Deere and Caterpillar run finance arms that generate roughly a fifth of their operating profit; Cisco and Dell do the same for enterprise hardware. (Blue Street Capital, 2026\) What's different, and riskier, is the closer historical parallel: the telecom vendor-financing boom of the late 1990s, when Lucent, Nortel and Cisco extended billions in credit so telecom carriers would buy their networking equipment. When the dot-com bubble burst, the carriers collapsed, and the vendors were left writing off billions in bad loans while demand for their own hardware evaporated at the same time. It was financing designed to manufacture its own customer base, and it worked, until it didn't.

**Are banks finished?**  
Not remotely, they've just moved up a layer. US banks now hold roughly $1.14tn in loans to non-bank financial institutions, up from about $250bn in 2010, a 23% annual growth rate that dwarfs the 4% growth in ordinary bank lending over the same period. (Forbes, 2026\) Rather than lending directly to a neocloud buying GPUs, a bank is increasingly likely to be funding the specialist lender who does. JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon warned in late 2025 that repeated defaults tend to signal more trouble beneath the surface, after two high-profile private credit defaults, without mentioning that JPMorgan and the other large US banks had spent nearly a decade quietly building exposure to the same ecosystem he was warning about. (Forbes, 2026\)

**So what happens if it unwinds?**  
Contracts are relatively new and untested through a downturn, and the lender of last resort in several of these deals is the same company selling the hardware. That is not automatically a problem, captive finance has underwritten entire industries before. But the last time a chip and equipment supplier financed its own customer base at this scale, the unwind took the supplier down with the customers. Whether this cycle ends differently depends on something nobody can fully price yet: how much of that $1.5tn financing gap turns out to be real demand, and how much of it turns out to be demand the financing itself created.

**How much is real demand?**  
Our evidence suggests not all. Much of the demand is incestuous investment where OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI, CoreWeave and others are trading billions. (Rediff, 2025\) The financing gap is not just being filled by lending, it is being inflated by cyclical investments and hyperscalers marking up their stakes in each other. One example of this is Google and Amazon aggressively increasing the value of their stakes in Anthropic. (DDR Innova, 2026\) These accounting practices extend to GPUs. Many are treating them as long term assets that depreciate gradually over ten years. (Clifford Chance, 2026\) Whilst Moore's law may no longer apply, the technological improvements are far from stagnant and realistically GPUs are becoming obsolete in a fraction of a decade. (Stanley Laman, 2025\) Even if the underlying demand for AI comes good, the payoff may outlast the maturity debt structures financing it. (SemiAnalysis, 2026\)
