**What is a Debt-Doom-Loop?**  
A Debt-Doom-Loop is a process where yields on government bonds rise due to the lenders: investment and central banks, pension funds and other institutions demanding higher payments due to more risky perceived returns. The loop becomes a problem when an economy's growth rate falls below the debt's interest rate causing the economy's debt burden to grow faster than it can service it. This causes spending on infrastructure and education to be crowded out as governments struggle to meet rising interest costs. Therefore, governments are forced to issue more bonds at even higher yields to finance these investments. This causes a vicious spiral where debt rises exponentially in the economy.

The 30-year yield of the sovereign bond market acts as a barometer for the fiscal risk in an economy. This is because significant premiums are demanded if lenders are locking up their capital for such a long time and believe that the government is entering the debt doom loop. We are currently seeing this play out across the globe. 30-year yields are reaching their highest points since the late 90s and 2000s with the US bond reaching 5.19%, (CNBC, 2026a) the UK gilt surging to roughly 5.7% (Bloomberg, 2026\) and Japan's reaching 4.185%. (Trading Economics, 2026\) In terms of the crowding out effect, in the UK, the cost of servicing government debt is forecast to remain over £109 billion, consuming about 8% of government spending. (House of Commons Library, 2026\) This debt is roughly equivalent to the entire budget of the Department of Education. In the past 20 years, debt servicing has tended to make up less than 55% of this budget.

**Why now?**  
The drivers of the current hike in yield rates are: uncertainty around inflation, forcing premiums higher to compensate; quantitative tightening, where central banks are net sellers rather than buyers; and the disappearance of reliable marginal buyers like Japanese life insurers and Chinese reserve managers. (Institute for Fiscal Studies, 2025; Bank of England, 2026\)

**When has this happened before?**  
In the UK, one of the most relevant examples is the UK Sterling Crisis of 1976\. Following years of stagflation, deteriorating productivity and mounting public sector deficits, confidence in the pound collapsed. Gilt yields spiked above 15% as investors demanded compensation for holding sterling-denominated debt and the government eventually resorted to a $3.9B loan from the IMF. (Chatham House, 2016; Economics Observatory, 2025\) With that came conditions forcing the government to adopt a fiscal philosophy of austerity, affecting housing, infrastructure and industrial investment, producing the crowding-out effect discussed earlier. The crisis also had huge long-term impacts on the UK. Having the IMF partially control fiscal policy was a humiliation for a G7 economy and damaged the Labour government's economic credibility, contributing to the Winter of Discontent and the 1979 election defeat. (OMFIF, 2016\)  
Ultimately, debt-doom-loops historically come with larger political scarring that can negatively affect the effectiveness of subsequent fiscal policy for years to come.

**What are the effects in this case?**  
The effects of a crisis on this scale would be similar in magnitude to the 2007 financial crisis. Unlike the 1976 incident, the impact would be global, not just limited to one nation. That means the IMF would not be able to bail out a crisis on this scale. What would have to happen is a period of extreme fiscal austerity. The problem is that no government wants to be remembered as the one that cut all spending.  
The other option is quantitative easing to manage debt. This is where governments print their currency, buy their own bonds to suppress interest rates and stop yields from rising. This comes with a massive weakening of the country's currency.  
Whether the solution is austerity or quantitative easing, there will be real costs for not only investors, but for everyone in society.
