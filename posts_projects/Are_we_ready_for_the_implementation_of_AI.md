The impacts of AI are huge, there is no debate there. Elon Musk, one of the most prominent figures in the AI trade, predicts that the thinking capacity of AI will exceed that of humanity in five years and dwarf it in ten. He expects that inevitably AIs will not take orders from people any more than humans would from chimpanzees (The Economist, 2026). While this may be possible, a completely different question is actually implementing this intelligence into society. A key requirement in the implementation of AI is that people actually want it. Contrary to the norm, Gen Z is not welcoming the new technology with open arms. While Gen Z is the group which uses AI tools the most, (London School of Economics, 2025), in a recent Gallup poll, only 18% say that they feel hopeful about the technology, down from 27% last year. (Gallup, 2026)
Not only that, though 56% say the tools help them finish work faster, eight in 10 admit that using AI in this way makes actual learning more difficult in the future. (The Verge, 2026)
I myself have experienced this phenomenon, cognitive offloading. Many people my age admit that they can barely focus on anything anymore. While some of this is just the result of 'doom-scrolling,' some of the blame must go to AI. At the end of the day, why would you spend longer than a minute trying to solve a problem when AI can do it for you in seconds? It may be difficult for the AI boom to continue to sustain itself if the generation meant to be accepting it is increasingly resentful of it.

The Mag-7 overlaid on the broader S&P 500 index is eerily beginning to look like the Dot Com bubble where the 4 horsemen (today the Mag-7) are dipping below the rest of the S&P 500. What followed in the case of the 4 horsemen is a later broader market crash of around USD 5 trillion (Forbes, 2026). Nowadays, the situation might even be worse, the technology giants take up around 35% of the US stock market, as opposed to roughly 15% in the case of the early 2000s. It is difficult to say whether this is another example of financial amnesia or whether for one of the first times ever in history, "this time will be different."

While the gains of AI, in a similar manner to the internet, are objectively massive and sure to structurally alter the workforce, that doesn't mean it will do this in the short term. This reflects Amara's Law, "We tend to overestimate the effect of a technology in the short run and underestimate the effect in the long run." Frankly, we may not be ready for the impacts of AI. I recently attended London Tech Week 2026, an event where it was incredibly difficult to have a conversation not about AI. There is no argument that there is a huge amount of hype around the technology. However, upon talking with a software developer who called himself an AI Consultant, he shared that all of his clients - small businesses - told him they wanted to use AI to completely overhaul their workflows. After a process of research and planning, the only application of AI that the majority found was a chatbot for their company website. This is one example suggesting a broader theme, everyone knows AI is revolutionary, but very few know what they want it to revolutionise.

There is no question that AI has and will continue to change the world. However, it may take far longer than some think for the benefits to truly materialise and until then, it would be wise to expect a correction of the market as AI companies continue to look more and more overvalued.
